Most of us know the name C.S. Lewis from the bookshelf. Clive Staples Lewis — British author, Oxford and Cambridge scholar, and Anglican layman — gave us The Chronicles of Narnia, The Screwtape Letters, and Mere Christianity. He was a close friend of J.R.R. Tolkien, and the two of them sat together in that famous Oxford circle, the Inklings. By his own account in his memoir, Surprised by Joy, Lewis was baptized as a child but drifted from his faith as a young man, returning to it only in his early thirties — and even that return owed much to Tolkien and a few other friends who would not let him go.

But the Lewis of those early years was no believer. He was an atheist — and not a quiet one. He had buried his mother when he was nine years old. He had survived the trenches of the First World War. And he looked out at a universe that struck him as cruel, unjust, and indifferent. In his own memoir he confessed the strange contradiction at the heart of his unbelief. He was, in his words, "very angry with God for not existing." Furious at a Being he insisted was not there.

Lewis began to wonder where his own outrage came from. If the universe were truly meaningless, why did injustice make him so angry? A man does not call a line crooked, he reasoned, unless he carries in his mind some idea of a straight line. His anger at the world's wrongness pointed to a standard of rightness he could not account for on his own. The very fury he aimed at God became the thread that led him back. He would later describe his conversion not as a glad homecoming but as a surrender, calling himself the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England. He did not walk into his newfound faith. He was dragged in, kicking and struggling.

Have you ever been angry with God? Have you ever carried thoughts of discontent toward Him?

If you have, do not believe your anger has pushed you outside of God's reach. The most reluctant convert in England got there by WAY of his rage. So today, let's talk about what it means to be angry with God — and what that anger might be trying to tell us.

I want to rehearse a story you have undoubtedly read before. You, like me, have probably had some strong reactions to it. You may have questioned why God would do what He did. Today we are going to take a deeper look at a story that, no matter who you are, tends to stir up strong emotion. Then, by contrast, we will read a second story — one that is strangely similar — to help us draw out some lessons as we bring the whole thing together.

The title of my message today is "The Tale of Two Carts."
⸻
The First Cart: Uzzah

2 Samuel 6:1–11

Again David gathered all the choice men of Israel, thirty thousand. And David arose and went with all the people who were with him from Baale Judah to bring up from there the ark of God, whose name is called by the Name, the LORD of Hosts, who dwells between the cherubim. So they set the ark of God on a new cart, and brought it out of the house of Abinadab, which was on the hill; and Uzzah and Ahio, the sons of Abinadab, drove the new cart. And they brought it out of the house of Abinadab, which was on the hill, accompanying the ark of God; and Ahio went before the ark. Then David and all the house of Israel played music before the LORD on all kinds of instruments of fir wood, on harps, on stringed instruments, on tambourines, on sistrums, and on cymbals. And when they came to Nachon's threshing floor, Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it, for the oxen stumbled. Then the anger of the LORD was aroused against Uzzah, and God struck him there for his error; and he died there by the ark of God. And David became angry because of the LORD's outbreak against Uzzah; and he called the name of the place Perez Uzzah to this day. David was afraid of the LORD that day; and he said, "How can the ark of the LORD come to me?" So David would not move the ark of the LORD with him into the City of David; but David took it aside into the house of Obed-Edom the Gittite. The ark of the LORD remained in the house of Obed-Edom the Gittite three months. And the LORD blessed Obed-Edom and all his household.

1 Chronicles 13:7–14

So they carried the ark of God on a new cart from the house of Abinadab, and Uzza and Ahio drove the cart. Then David and all Israel played music before God with all their might, with singing, on harps, on stringed instruments, on tambourines, on cymbals, and with trumpets. And when they came to Chidon's threshing floor, Uzza put out his hand to hold the ark, for the oxen stumbled. Then the anger of the LORD was aroused against Uzza, and He struck him because he put his hand to the ark; and he died there before God. And David became angry because of the LORD's outbreak against Uzza; therefore that place is called Perez Uzza to this day. David was afraid of God that day, saying, "How can I bring the ark of God to me?" So David would not move the ark with him into the City of David, but took it aside into the house of Obed-Edom the Gittite. The ark of God remained with the family of Obed-Edom in his house three months. And the LORD blessed the house of Obed-Edom and all that he had.

This is the first cart story — a story of a rash action and a strong reaction. Uzzah was no stranger to the ark. It had stood in his family's house since the days of Samuel, long enough that Scripture measures one stretch of it at twenty years (1 Sam 7:1–2), and by David's day a generation had come and gone. Uzzah grew up with it. He grew up with the stories that surrounded it, and with the responsibility of caring for it.

But God had not left the handling of holy things to instinct. In Numbers 4 we read part of His instruction to those charged with the most holy objects. The holy things were not to be touched, lest the one who touched them die. They were not even to be looked upon as they were covered, lest the onlooker die (Num 4:15, 19–20). The ark itself had been fashioned with rings and carrying poles for this very reason — so that it could be moved without a single hand ever touching it.

Exodus 25:12–15

You shall cast four rings of gold for it, and put them in its four corners; two rings shall be on one side, and two rings on the other side. And you shall make poles of acacia wood, and overlay them with gold. You shall put the poles into the rings on the sides of the ark, that the ark may be carried by them. The poles shall be in the rings of the ark; they shall not be taken from it.

The expectation was clear. The instructions were immutable.

So why does the story provoke such anger in us? Simply put, because Uzzah's reach feels like such a small infraction. He cared for the ark. He saw it slip, and he worried something might happen to it. He acted quickly, without thinking — the most natural reaction in the world. Did he deserve to die for it?

Hold that question. Let's keep walking.

The Second Cart: The Philistines

1 Samuel 4:6–7

Now when the Philistines heard the noise of the shout, they said, "What does the sound of this great shout in the camp of the Hebrews mean?" Then they understood that the ark of the LORD had come into the camp.

1 Samuel 5:10–12

Therefore they sent the ark of God to Ekron. So it was, as the ark of God came to Ekron, that the Ekronites cried out, saying, "They have brought the ark of the God of Israel to us, to kill us and our people!" So they sent and gathered together all the lords of the Philistines, and said, "Send away the ark of the God of Israel, and let it go back to its own place, so that it does not kill us and our people." For there was a deadly destruction throughout all the city; the hand of God was very heavy there. And the men who did not die were stricken with the tumors, and the cry of the city went up to heaven.

1 Samuel 6:1

Now the ark of the LORD was in the country of the Philistines seven months.

1 Samuel 6:7–12

Now therefore, make a new cart, take two milk cows which have never been yoked, and hitch the cows to the cart; and take their calves home, away from them. Then take the ark of the LORD and set it on the cart; and put the articles of gold which you are returning to Him as a trespass offering in a chest by its side. Then send it away, and let it go. And watch: if it goes up the road to its own territory, to Beth Shemesh, then He has done us this great evil. But if not, then we shall know that it is not His hand that struck us—it happened to us by chance."

Then the men did so; they took two milk cows and hitched them to the cart, and shut up their calves at home. And they set the ark of the LORD on the cart, and the chest with the gold rats and the images of their tumors. Then the cows headed straight for the road to Beth Shemesh, and went along the highway, lowing as they went, and did not turn aside to the right hand or the left. And the lords of the Philistines went after them to the border of Beth Shemesh.

Now here is the second cart — and this is where the story turns strange.

Years earlier, the Philistines had captured the ark in battle. They were not trained in how to handle it; they had no priests of Israel, no scroll of Numbers, no instructions at all. When you seize a treasured spoil of war, you don't stop to read the manual on how to transport it. You grab it and go. And there was a great deal of grabbing — a great deal of handling — to get that ark onto a cart and on its way.

Yet the Philistines had learned something. God had taught them, through death and disease, that this ark did not belong among them (1 Sam 5:10–12). They were not fully convinced it WAS the God of Israel at work — notice how carefully they hedge, saying that if the cows go straight home then it was His hand, and if not, it was only chance. But they were not willing to take any chances. So after seven months (1 Sam 6:1), they built a new cart, set the ark on it, and sent it home (1 Sam 6:7–12).

Here is the contrast that gives this message its name. Two carts. Two groups of people moving the same holy ark in the same way. The Philistines — ignorant pagans who handled it carelessly — were sent home in peace. Uzzah — who grew up beside it, who knew it was holy — reached out one hand to steady it and fell dead. David himself was angry that day, angry before he was ever afraid, and he named the place Perez-Uzzah, "the outbreak against Uzzah" (2 Sam 6:8).

And before we leave the second cart, I want you to see how that journey ended, because most of us stop reading too soon. The cart rolled into a barley field at Beth Shemesh, and the people there were overjoyed. They were Israelites. They were in a Levitical town. And then this:

1 Samuel 6:19–20

Then He struck the men of Beth Shemesh, because they had looked into the ark of the LORD... and the people lamented because the LORD had struck the people with a great slaughter. And the men of Beth Shemesh said, "Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God? And to whom shall it go up from us?"

The Philistines pushed that cart to the border and walked home alive. The Israelites who received it did not. Same ark, same day, same road — and the ones who died were the ones who should have known better.

How is that fair?

That is the question. And the answer asks more of us than it asks of God.


What the Two Carts Teach Us

How much faith do we really have? That, it turns out, is where the answer begins.

1. True faith trusts God to be God

Proverbs 3:5–6

Trust in the LORD with all your heart, 
And lean not on your own understanding; 
In all your ways acknowledge Him, 
And He shall direct your paths.

Before we can talk about Uzzah's hand, we have to talk about the cart it was resting on. Where did Israel get the idea to move the ark on a cart at all? They got it from the Philistines. The one method Scripture never authorized was the one method the pagans had used — and Israel copied it. This was not a moment of rebellion; it was a moment of borrowed convenience. Somebody said, "Here is how it was done last time. It worked. Let's do that."

And look at how the decision was made. David consulted the captains of thousands and hundreds and every leader, and he asked the assembly, "If it seems good to you, and if it is of the LORD our God..." (1 Chr 13:1–3). He took a poll. He built a consensus. Thirty thousand men agreed. Everyone was singing. The mood was wonderful. And the whole procession was already outside the will of God before it ever reached the threshing floor. Sincerity is not a substitute for obedience. A crowd is not a substitute for a command. You can have unity, enthusiasm, good music, and a pure motive, and still be doing a holy thing in an unholy way.

So what would true faith have looked like for Uzzah? He could have walked beside that cart believing that if the ark began to fall, the God who dwelt above it was perfectly capable of keeping it from ever hitting the ground. He could have said, "God told me not to touch it — so I will obey, and I will leave the rest to Him."

And Uzzah, of all people, had the evidence. The stories that traveled with that ark into his father's house were stories of a God who defends His own honor without help. That ark had sat overnight in the temple of Dagon, and in the morning the idol was face down on the floor. They stood him up again. The next morning he was face down again, with his head and his hands broken off on the threshold (1 Sam 5:3–4). No Israelite lifted a finger. God toppled a false god in that god's own house, in enemy territory, with nobody there to defend Him.

That is the question this story presses on us: was it ever Uzzah's job to stand in for an absent God? The Lord of Israel did not need a steadying hand. And neither does He need ours. How much of our anxiety is really this same reach — the fear that if we do not intervene, God's work will topple? We manage outcomes He never asked us to manage. We tell the small lie to protect the big truth. We bend a command because we can see, plainly, that circumstances require it. That is the cart of our own making, and the hand is already halfway out. Trusting God to be God means obeying Him at exactly the point where we cannot see how obedience will work out. Anywhere else, it isn't trust — it's agreement.

2. Our nature must change — not God's

Isaiah 55:8–9

"For My thoughts are not your thoughts, 
Nor are your ways My ways," says the LORD. 
"For as the heavens are higher than the earth, 
So are My ways higher than your ways, 
And My thoughts than your thoughts."

Ephesians 4:22–24

that you put off, concerning your former conduct, the old man which grows corrupt according to the deceitful lusts, and be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and that you put on the new man which was created according to God, in true righteousness and holiness.

Uzzah's hand moved before his mind did. We called that natural, and it was. But that is exactly the lesson. In that moment God teaches us that our nature, left to itself, is not right.

Think about what we usually mean when we defend a reaction by calling it natural. We mean it came from the truest part of us, the part underneath the manners and the effort — and we assume that because it was unrehearsed, it must have been sincere, and because it was sincere, it must have been good. Uzzah's reach was all three. It was natural, it was unrehearsed, and it was sincere. And it was wrong. Sincerity tells you where a reaction came from. It tells you nothing about whether it should have come at all.

Notice too that a reflex is not something you decide in the moment. A reflex is what has already been decided, long before the moment arrives. When the oxen stumbled, Uzzah did not weigh Numbers 4 against the situation and choose badly. He never got that far. His hand was out before the thought could form. Which means the real question is not what he did at the threshing floor, but what had been forming in him during all those quiet years in his father's house. In the moment of crisis, we do not rise to the level of our intentions. We fall to the level of what we have already become.

So the answer is not to ask God to lower His holiness to fit our instincts. That is the request underneath most of our anger — not that God would explain Himself, but that He would revise Himself, that He would come down to a standard we can already meet without changing. He will not. The answer is for our instincts to be made new — to put off the old self and put on the new, "created according to God, in true righteousness and holiness."

Romans 12:2

And do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, that you may prove what is that good and acceptable and perfect will of God.

That is not the work of a moment at an altar. That is the work of years — Scripture read until it forms the reflex, obedience practiced in small things until it is no longer a decision. We are not polishing the old man. We are burying him and putting on the new. And what is at stake is not merely our behavior when we are thinking clearly. It is what our hands do when we are not thinking at all.

3. God deals with people according to His plans

Romans 9:15–16

For He says to Moses, "I will have mercy on whomever I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whomever I will have compassion." So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy.

God does not treat every person in the same way, because He is working a plan far larger than the single moment we can see.

But look closely at the two carts and you will see that this is not arbitrary. There is a pattern in it, and the pattern is not the one our anger expects. The Philistines had no covenant, no law, no priesthood, and no revelation. God dealt with them patiently — He taught them with tumors and toppled idols over seven months until even pagans could work out that this ark had to go home. He was instructing people who had never been told. But the men of Beth Shemesh had been told. Uzzah had been told. And the judgment fell there.

Luke 12:48

For everyone to whom much is given, from him much will be required; and to whom much has been committed, of him they will ask the more.

Amos 3:2

You only have I known of all the families of the earth; 
Therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.

That is a sobering way to read a familiar promise. Amos does not say, "You only have I known, therefore you get a pass." He says the opposite. Nearness to God raises the standard rather than lowering it. So when we ask why the pagans got mercy and the covenant people got judgment, the answer is that they were not being measured by the same light. God was gentle with the ignorant and severe with the informed.

There is also the matter of timing. This was not a private accident on a country road. Thirty thousand men were watching. The ark had been out of its place for a generation; a new king was consolidating a kingdom; a nation was about to be taught how it would approach its God for centuries to come. God dealt with Uzzah at the founding moment of Israel's worship so that all Israel — and all of us reading it three thousand years later — might know that He is God, and not to be handled lightly. One man died at a threshing floor, and a nation learned to fear the Lord. We see the outbreak. We do not see the ledger.

And there is a mercy hiding in this section that we usually miss entirely. The same three months that terrified David were three months of blessing for Obed-Edom, who housed that very ark. Identical object. Opposite outcome. Death at Perez-Uzzah, blessing at Obed-Edom's house, and no explanation offered for either. To be angry that God treats one person differently than another is to claim that we could sit in His chair and sort it out better. We could not.

4. Our lives belong to God

1 Corinthians 6:19–20

Or do you not know that your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought at a price; therefore glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which are God's.

And beneath it all lies this: we are not our own.

Ask why the ark was holy. Not because of the acacia wood. Not because of the gold, or the craftsmanship, or the age of it. That box was holy for one reason: it belonged to God, and He had put His presence there. Holiness was never a property of the material. It was a matter of ownership.

Now carry that straight across. Paul says your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit. The same logic that made a wooden chest untouchable now applies to you — not because of anything impressive in the material, but because of who owns it and who dwells in it. We are quick to claim the comfort in that verse and slow to claim the weight of it. If we belong to Him, then He may deal with us at His pleasure, spend us as He sees fit, and give us seasons we did not choose. He is not taking something from us when He does. He is exercising a title we already conceded.

Hebrews 10:19–22

Therefore, brethren, having boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh... let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith.

Do you see it? Uzzah could not touch the ark and live. We are invited into the Most Holy Place itself, and told to come with boldness. Not because God's holiness was lowered — it never has been — but because the price was paid by Someone else. So the same verse that says "you are not your own" also tells us what we cost Him. Our lives belong to God twice over: He made us, and then He bought us back. The story of Uzzah is not there to make us cower at a distance. It is there to make us understand what it took to bring us near.

There is one more thing in this account that we rarely preach, and it is the piece that makes all of this practical.

David was angry, and then David was afraid, and the ark sat in another man's house for three months while he thought it over. But he did not stay there. When he heard that Obed-Edom was being blessed, he went back — and this time he went back to the book.

1 Chronicles 15:2

Then David said, "No one may carry the ark of God but the Levites, for the LORD has chosen them to carry the ark of God and to minister before Him forever."

1 Chronicles 15:13

For because you did not do it the first time, the LORD our God broke out against us, because we did not consult Him about the proper order.

1 Chronicles 15:15

And the children of the Levites bore the ark of God on their shoulders, by its poles, as Moses had commanded according to the word of the LORD.

Read that thirteenth verse again, because David is not blaming God. He is not blaming Uzzah. He says we — we did not consult Him about the proper order. The king takes it on himself. And then he does the one thing that fixed it: he found out what God had actually said, and he did that instead.

So the third time the ark moved, there was no cart. There were Levites, and poles, and shoulders, and a procession that stopped every six paces to offer sacrifice, and a king dancing in a linen ephod, and it arrived in Jerusalem with joy. The answer to a tale of two carts turned out to be no cart at all.

That is what anger is supposed to do. It asked its hard question, it drove David to the Scriptures, and it came out the other side as obedience and joy.


Closing

So we return to that threshing floor — to the cart, to the outstretched hand. Uzzah reached out to steady the ark, and he fell. And if we are honest, something in us flinches. Something in us wants to say what David said that day — David, who was angry before he was ever afraid: That isn't fair. He was only trying to help.

Our anger asked, what kind of God does this? And the answer came back in pieces.

It came first as a question about faith. Uzzah had lived alongside the ark; it had stood in his father's house for years. But familiarity is not faith. Nearness is not reverence. The Lord Himself once asked whether, when the Son of Man returns, He will find faith on the earth at all (Luke 18:8). So we have to ask it of ourselves — not whether we are near the holy things, not whether we grew up beside the church, the brethren, and the Scriptures, but whether what we carry is true faith, or only the comfortable habit of religion.

Then it came as a word about our nature. Uzzah's hand moved before his mind did. It was instinct — and that is exactly the problem. Our instincts, left unchanged, reach for control, reach to manage God, reach in anger. We cannot polish the old nature into something holy. It has to be made new. The clenched fist of anger has to become the open hand of surrender.

And it came as a hard truth about the freedom of God. He does not deal with every person in the same way, because He is working a plan larger than the single moment we can see. We stand at Perez-Uzzah and see only the outburst. We do not see the whole purpose. To be angry that God treats one person differently than another is to assume we could sit in His chair and do it better. We cannot.

Which leaves the truth beneath all the others — the one Uzzah's story was pressing toward from the start. Our lives are not our own. The ark was holy because it belonged to God, and so do we. We were bought, we are kept, and we are His. The anger we began with was really the protest of a heart that still believed its life belonged to itself.

So remember the young atheist, C.S. Lewis — the man who was angry at God for not existing. His fury turned out to be the thread that led him home. And remember David, who was angry at a threshing floor and ended up dancing before the ark three months later, because his anger sent him back to what God had said.

Let your anger do its honest work. Let it ask its hard questions. But do not let it stop at the threshing floor. Let it lead you past the outstretched hand to the open one — the hand that finally lets go and says: I am not my own. My life belongs to You.

That is what true faith looks like. May God find it in us when He comes.
